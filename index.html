Getting Started

Indonesian for Beginners: A Friendly Starting Guide

Bahasa Indonesia — the Indonesian language — is the official language of Indonesia, the world's fourth most populous country, and a second language for well over 150 million people across the archipelago. If you have been putting off learning a new language because of horror stories about verb tables and unfamiliar scripts, Indonesian is the pleasant surprise waiting for you: it uses the same Latin alphabet as English, and its grammar is famously forgiving.

No new alphabet, no verb tables

Two famous facts make Indonesian beginners relax. First, you already know the alphabet — Indonesian is written in ordinary Latin letters, so street signs in Jakarta, menus in Bali and messages from Indonesian friends are readable from day one. Second, verbs never change their form for person or tense. The verb makan (to eat) stays makan whether it is "I eat", "she ate" or "they will eat"; small helper words like sudah (already), sedang (in the middle of doing) and akan (will) carry the time meaning instead.

How Indonesian sentences are built

The word order will feel comfortingly familiar: subject, verb, object, just like English. "I eat rice" is Saya makan nasi — word for word the same order. One difference to enjoy rather than fear: the describing word usually follows the thing it describes, so "red car" is mobil merah (car red) and "big house" is rumah besar. Our sentence-building guide explains the whole system calmly.

The first words to learn

Start with the words you will use every hour: halo (hello), terima kasih (thank you), tolong (please / help), air (water), makanan (food), berapa? (how much?) and di mana? (where?). Add the numbers one to ten and the question words, and you can already manage taxis, food stalls and market shopping. Our everyday vocabulary guide collects sixty of the most useful words in one place.

Pronunciation is kind

Indonesian spelling is almost perfectly phonetic: letters are pronounced the way they are written, and each letter keeps its sound. There are only a few traps for English speakers — the letter "c" is always pronounced "ch", and a final "k" is a soft stop in the throat. Our pronunciation guide covers every letter in a few minutes.

A realistic study routine

Fifteen minutes a day beats a monthly marathon. Spend five minutes on new words, five reading real Indonesian — menus, signs, song titles — and five speaking aloud. Use the translator on this site to check sentences about your own life: your family, your work, your town. Personal sentences stick in memory far better than textbook ones.

Above all, speak from the first week. Indonesians are famously patient and delighted when a visitor tries their language. A cheerful terima kasih at a warung food stall will teach you more about Indonesian warmth than a chapter of grammar. Selamat belajar — happy learning!

Pronunciation

Indonesian Pronunciation and Spelling: Every Letter Explained

Indonesian may be the most honestly spelled language you will ever meet. A spelling reform in 1972 tidied the system up, and today the rule is simple: say what you see. Once you know the handful of letters whose sounds differ from English, you can read any Indonesian word aloud — a menu, a signboard, a name — and be understood.

The vowels

There are six vowel sounds, written a, i, u, e, o — with "e" doing double duty. The vowels a, i, u and o are pure and steady, much like in Spanish or Italian: nasi (rice) is "nah-see", susu (milk) is "soo-soo", kopi (coffee) is "koh-pee". Avoid the sliding diphthong sounds of English — Indonesian vowels stay single and clean.

The famous letter e

The letter "e" represents two sounds. One is the clear "eh" of enak (delicious — "eh-nahk") and besok (tomorrow — "beh-sok"). The other is the soft, muffled "uh" (schwa) of selamat (greetings — "seh-lah-maht") and terima in terima kasih. Dictionaries sometimes mark the schwa as ê, but everyday writing does not. The good news: Indonesians understand either sound, so this is a polish detail, not a barrier.

Consonants that differ from English

Four things to remember. First, c is always "ch": cinta (love) is "cheen-tah", never "sin-tah". Second, the combinations ng and ny are single sounds: uang (money) ends with the "ng" of "sing", and nyonya (Mrs) begins like the "ny" in "canyon". Third, a final k is a gentle stop in the throat, so tidak (no) ends abruptly: "tee-dah(k)". Fourth, the letter r is lightly rolled or tapped, as in Spanish.

Stress and rhythm

Indonesian stress is gentle and falls on the second-to-last syllable: te-RI-ma KA-sih, se-LA-mat PA-gi. There are no tones — unlike Thai or Mandarin, raising or lowering your pitch never changes a word's meaning. Speak in an even, relaxed rhythm and you will sound natural quickly.

A reading warm-up

Try these everyday words aloud: selamat pagi (good morning), apa kabar (how are you), berapa harganya (how much is it), jalan-jalan (a stroll, sightseeing) and hati-hati (be careful). Notice how doubling a word is written with a hyphen — that is Indonesian's friendly way of making plurals and leisurely meanings.

Keep practising with real sentences from the translator and the phrasebook, where tricky phrases carry pronunciation notes. Within a week, Indonesian print will stop looking foreign and start sounding like language.

Doubled letters and gentle endings

Two last details polish your reading. Doubled vowels almost never occur, but doubled consonants in the middle of a word are pronounced with a tiny pause — and the letter combination kh in words of Arabic origin, like khusus (special), is a soft breathy "h" at the back of the throat. Word endings stay light: never swallow a final syllable the way English does. Read slowly, pronounce every letter, and Indonesians will follow you easily — then speed up naturally as the words become familiar.

Better Results

How to Translate English to Indonesian Accurately

Machine translation between English and Indonesian is among the best in the world — the languages share an alphabet and a similar sentence order. Even so, small habits on your side make the difference between Indonesian that merely makes sense and Indonesian that sounds like a person wrote it. These nine tips come from the errors we see most often.

1. Write complete sentences

Fragments give the translator too little to work with. "Going tomorrow" could be a question, a plan or an order. Write "I am going tomorrow" and the Indonesian comes out clean: Saya pergi besok. Full sentences also help the tool pick the right helper words — sudah (already), sedang (currently) and akan (will) — that carry time in Indonesian.

2. Keep sentences reasonably short

One long English sentence with three commas often becomes one tangled Indonesian sentence. Split long thoughts into two or three shorter ones before translating; the output improves immediately, and you can always rejoin the sentences afterwards.

3. Use punctuation properly

Full stops and question marks tell the translator where ideas end and what kind of sentence it is. A question mark matters: Indonesian questions often begin with apakah or end with the softening particle -kah, and the tool chooses them when it can see you are asking.

4. Avoid slang and idioms

"Break a leg" or "it's raining cats and dogs" translated word by word produces nonsense in any language. Rewrite idioms plainly first — "good luck", "it is raining heavily" — then translate. The meaning survives; the confusion does not.

5. Decide how polite you need to be

English "you" becomes informal kamu or respectful Anda, and "I" becomes neutral saya or casual aku. Machine output usually picks the safe, polite forms. If you are writing to a close friend, you may want the casual ones; if you are writing to an office or an elder, keep the polite ones. Our greetings guide explains the levels.

6. Be careful with names and places

Translators sometimes translate names that happen to be ordinary words. If a person's or company's name must stay exactly as it is, translate the sentence in two parts and paste the name back in afterwards.

7. Watch English words with several meanings

"Book" the object (buku) and "book" the action (memesan) are different Indonesian words. If a result looks odd, add a clarifying word in English — "book a ticket", "read a book" — and translate again.

8. Translate paragraph by paragraph

Long pasted documents drift: names get translated two ways, and terms wander. Work one paragraph at a time, and keep a note of names and technical terms so they stay consistent from start to finish.

9. Back-translate to check

Finally, paste the Indonesian result back into the translator in the Indonesian-to-English direction. If the English that returns matches what you meant, your translation is safe to send. Try it now on the translator — and keep our travel phrases handy if your translation is for a trip to Indonesia.

Travel

Essential Indonesian Phrases for Travelling in Indonesia

Indonesia is a traveller's gift: one language works across more than 17,000 islands. English will carry you through Bali's resorts, but step into a local food stall in Yogyakarta, a bus station in Sumatra or a market in Sulawesi and a little Indonesian changes everything — prices drop, directions appear, and invitations follow. Here are the phrases that matter most, grouped by the moments you will actually use them.

Greetings and courtesy

Match your greeting to the clock: Selamat pagi (good morning, until about 11), Selamat siang (midday), Selamat sore (late afternoon) and Selamat malam (evening and night). Ask Apa kabar? (how are you?) and answer Baik-baik saja (I am fine). Thank everyone with terima kasih — it is the hardest-working phrase in the country.

Getting around

For ojeks (motorbike taxis), cars and buses: agree the price first with Berapa tarifnya? (how much is the fare?). Directions: belok kiri (turn left), belok kanan (turn right), jalan terus (go straight) and berhenti di sini (stop here). The question Di mana...? (where is...?) plus any place name — stasiun (station), bandara (airport), pelabuhan (harbour) — will get you pointed the right way.

Food and drink

Eat where the locals eat: the warung, a small family food stall. Point and ask Ini apa? (what is this?), order with Saya mau nasi goreng (I would like fried rice), and manage the chilli with tidak pedas (not spicy) or sedikit pedas (a little spicy). Praise the cook with Enak sekali! (very delicious!) and ask for the bill with Minta tagihannya.

Shopping and bargaining

Berapa harganya? (how much is it?) opens every negotiation. In markets, smiling bargaining is expected: Bisa kurang? (can it be less?) is the gentle classic. Close the deal with Baik, saya ambil ini (okay, I'll take this), or walk away kindly with Tidak apa-apa, terima kasih (it's fine, thank you). Our shopping guide goes deeper.

Emergencies and help

Memorise three lines just in case: Tolong! (help!), Panggil dokter (call a doctor) and Di mana rumah sakit? (where is the hospital?). Indonesians are famously quick to help a traveller in trouble — often before you finish the sentence.

Save this page, copy the phrases you need from our phrasebook, and use the translator for anything else that comes up on the road. Selamat jalan! — safe travels!

One phrase for every awkward moment

When language fails completely, Indonesians have a beloved escape hatch: a smile and the words Maaf, saya tidak mengerti (sorry, I don't understand), followed by pointing, showing a map, or opening the translator. Nobody will be offended; more often than not, a small crowd will gather to solve your problem together. Travel in Indonesia runs on this goodwill — the phrases above simply help you meet it halfway.

Numbers & Counting

Indonesian Numbers 1 to 100: Counting Made Simple

Numbers are among the first things you need in any language. In Indonesia you will use them for ojek fares, market prices, hotel rooms, phone numbers and bus times — often several times a day. The good news is that Indonesian counting is one of the most regular systems in the world: learn eleven words and a pattern, and you can build every number up to a million.

The numbers 1 to 10

Everything starts here: satu (one), dua (two), tiga (three), empat (four), lima (five), enam (six), tujuh (seven), delapan (eight), sembilan (nine) and sepuluh (ten). Zero is nol. Say them aloud a few times; vendors will recognise your effort instantly.

The teens and the tens

The teens add belas: sebelas (eleven), dua belas (twelve), tiga belas (thirteen) and so on up to sembilan belas (nineteen). The tens add puluh: dua puluh (twenty), tiga puluh (thirty), empat puluh (forty), up to sembilan puluh (ninety). Numbers in between simply join: twenty-five is dua puluh lima — twenty, five.

Hundreds, thousands and millions

Hundreds use ratus: seratus (one hundred), dua ratus (two hundred), lima ratus (five hundred). Thousands use ribu: seribu (one thousand), sepuluh ribu (ten thousand), seratus ribu (one hundred thousand). A million is satu juta. Notice how se- means "one" at the front of these blocks: seratus, seribu, sebelas, sepuluh.

Numbers in daily life

Indonesian prices come with many zeros — a meal might cost dua puluh lima ribu (twenty-five thousand rupiah). Locals often drop the word ribu in speech: "dua lima" can mean 25,000 in a price conversation, so listen for context. The key question is always Berapa harganya? (how much is it?). Phone numbers are read digit by digit, and room numbers are usually read in pairs.

Practise by counting real things this week — steps, stops, glasses of es teh — and test any number sentence in the translator before you use it. Numbers plus the phrases in our shopping guide make market day much easier.

A note on big prices

New visitors often freeze when a hotel quotes satu juta lima ratus ribu — one and a half million rupiah sounds like a fortune until you convert it and relax. Practise hearing the blocks: juta (millions), then ribu (thousands), then ratus (hundreds). Indonesians will happily write the number down for you — ask Bisa ditulis? (can you write it?) — and checking figures in the translator before you pay is a habit even experienced travellers keep.

Time & Dates

Days, Months and Telling the Time in Indonesian

"When?" is one of the most practical questions in any language. Whether you are catching a train in Java, fixing a meeting in Jakarta or planning around the rainy season, you need days, dates and clock times. Indonesian keeps this simple: a small set of words covers almost every plan you will make.

The seven days

The week runs: Senin (Monday), Selasa (Tuesday), Rabu (Wednesday), Kamis (Thursday), Jumat (Friday), Sabtu (Saturday) and Minggu (Sunday). Several came into Indonesian through Arabic centuries ago. Minggu is a double-duty word: it also means "week", so minggu depan is "next week" and hari Minggu is Sunday itself.

Today, tomorrow and yesterday

Three words handle most planning: hari ini (today), besok (tomorrow) and kemarin (yesterday). Add lusa (the day after tomorrow) and you have a tidy set. "See you tomorrow" is Sampai jumpa besok — a phrase worth using daily.

Parts of the day

The day divides into pagi (morning, roughly until 11), siang (late morning to mid-afternoon), sore (late afternoon until sunset) and malam (evening and night). Greetings follow exactly the same pattern — Selamat pagi, Selamat siang, Selamat sore, Selamat malam — which is why our greetings guide starts with the clock.

Months of the year

Indonesian month names will look pleasantly familiar: Januari, Februari, Maret, April, Mei, Juni, Juli, Agustus, September, Oktober, November, Desember. Dates are written day-first: 17 Agustus 2026 is 17 August 2026 — Indonesia's Independence Day. A year is tahun, so "next year" is tahun depan.

Asking and telling the time

Ask Jam berapa sekarang? (what time is it now?). The word jam means clock, hour and watch all at once. Answers use jam plus the number: jam sepuluh (ten o'clock). Half past uses setengah: setengah sebelas is half past ten — literally "half eleven", counting towards the coming hour, so be careful. Minutes past use lewat and minutes to use kurang: jam tiga lewat sepuluh (3:10), jam empat kurang lima (3:55, "four minus five").

A friendly warning: jam karet

Indonesians joke about jam karet — "rubber time" — the relaxed stretching of schedules in social life. Trains and flights keep strict time, but a friend's "sebentar lagi" (in a moment) may stretch. Knowing the words above helps you ask precise questions when precision matters.

Combine these with the numbers guide and you can read any timetable in the country. For longer sentences, draft them in the translator and check the word order before you travel.

Days in everyday phrases

A few ready-made expressions cover most real conversations: setiap hari (every day), hari kerja (a working day), akhir pekan (the weekend — literally "end of the week"), and hari libur (a holiday, a day off). To ask about a plan, Kapan? alone does the job, and to fix one, answer with day plus part of day: Hari Jumat, sore — Friday, late afternoon. Simple, and understood everywhere.

Family

Family Words in Indonesian: Relatives and Relationships

Family sits at the centre of Indonesian life, and the language reflects it: family words are used constantly — not only for actual relatives, but as warm, respectful ways to address almost anyone. Learning these words does more than build vocabulary: it explains why the food-stall owner calls you kak and why every older lady is somebody's tante.

Parents and grandparents

Father is ayah and mother is ibu. Grandfather is kakek and grandmother is nenek — combine them and kakek-nenek means grandparents. The word for family itself is keluarga, and you will see it everywhere, from keluarga besar (the extended family) to restaurant signs promising family-sized portions.

Brothers and sisters: kakak and adik

Indonesian does not have one word for "brother" and one for "sister". Instead it asks: older or younger? An elder sibling of either sex is kakak; a younger sibling of either sex is adik. When the sex matters, add it: kakak perempuan (elder sister), adik laki-laki (younger brother). This is not a detail — it is the pair of words you will hear most often in any Indonesian home.

Uncles, aunts and cousins

An uncle is paman and an aunt is bibi in textbook Indonesian, but daily life adds two borrowed favourites: om and tante, from Dutch, used for parents' friends as much as for real relatives. A cousin is sepupu. Because regional languages layer on their own kinship words, do not be surprised to hear other terms in Javanese or Sundanese homes — the national words above are understood everywhere.

Children and the next generation

A child is anak — a word that also does duty in dozens of compounds, from anak-anak (children) to anak muda (young people). A son is anak laki-laki and a daughter is anak perempuan. A husband is suami and a wife is istri; a grandchild is cucu.

Family words for strangers

Here is the charming part. In daily life, ibu (shortened to bu) and bapak (shortened to pak) are the polite way to address any adult woman or man — "excuse me, ma'am" is Permisi, Bu. Young adults are greeted as kakak or kak, and children call older family friends om and tante. Use these and you will sound instantly at home.

Talking about your family

Two sentence frames cover most introductions. "This is my mother" is Ini ibu saya. "I have two younger siblings" is Saya punya dua adik. Notice saya (I, my) sitting quietly at the end — the possessive follows the thing possessed, as our self-introduction guide shows in action.

Family questions are friendly conversation in Indonesia, not prying — asking about someone's keluarga shows you care. Practise your answers in the translator, and you will be ready for the warmest small talk in Southeast Asia.

Speaking

Indonesian Greetings and Goodbyes for Every Occasion

Every conversation in Indonesia begins and ends with warmth, and the words are easy to learn. Master this small set of greetings and goodbyes and you will never be stuck at the start or the end of a chat again — in a home, a shop, an office or on the phone.

The selamat family

The word selamat means safe, well and congratulations all at once, and it builds most Indonesian greetings. Match it to the clock: Selamat pagi (good morning), Selamat siang (good day, around midday), Selamat sore (good late afternoon) and Selamat malam (good evening, good night). The same word welcomes guests — Selamat datang (welcome) — and powers every congratulation in our festivals guide. Plain halo works any time, especially on the phone and among younger people.

Asking how someone is

The standard question is Apa kabar? — literally "what news?". The classic answer is Baik-baik saja (fine, all good) or simply Kabar baik (good news). Among friends you will also hear Gimana? (how's it going?) — the casual short form of bagaimana (how).

Meeting someone new

Introduce yourself with Nama saya... (my name is...) and seal the meeting with Senang bertemu dengan Anda (pleased to meet you) — or, between new friends, Senang berkenalan denganmu. A handshake is usual, often soft, sometimes with the right hand touching the heart afterwards as a sign of sincerity. Our introduction guide builds the full first conversation.

Politeness softeners

Three small words oil every interaction: tolong when asking for something, silakan when offering or inviting ("please, go ahead"), and permisi when passing by, entering, or interrupting politely. Add maaf (sorry) for the small bumps of daily life, and terima kasih said often and with a smile.

Saying goodbye

The everyday goodbye is Sampai jumpa (until we meet) — add a time and it becomes Sampai jumpa besok (see you tomorrow) or Sampai jumpa lagi (see you again). The formal pair distinguishes who is leaving: the one going says Selamat tinggal (goodbye, to those staying) and hears Selamat jalan (safe journey, to the one going) in return. Between friends, a cheerful Dah! — from the Dutch dag — is common in cities.

Take care

Finally, the warmest parting of all: Hati-hati di jalan (take care on the road). You will hear it from hosts, drivers and grandmothers across the country — and returning it is the perfect way to end any Indonesian conversation.

Try one greeting today — at a shop, with a neighbour, anywhere — and build the habit with the phrasebook. For meeting someone properly for the first time, continue to our self-introduction guide.

Shopping

Shopping and Bargaining in Indonesian: Market Phrases That Work

Indonesia's markets — the batik halls of Yogyakarta, the floating markets of Kalimantan, the art markets of Ubud — run on friendly talk. Prices in traditional markets are often a conversation, not a label, while malls and supermarkets use fixed prices marked harga pas. These phrases will help you shop with confidence and bargain with a smile, which is exactly how it is done locally.

Entering and looking around

Start with a greeting — Selamat pagi — and address the seller politely as Pak (sir) or Bu (ma'am). If you are just browsing, say Lihat-lihat dulu, ya (I'm just looking first) — sellers hear this all day and will let you browse in peace. To ask for something specific: Ada...? (do you have...?) followed by the item, as in Ada ukuran besar? (do you have a large size?). The word for shop is toko and for market, pasar.

Asking the price

The one question you will use everywhere: Berapa harganya? (how much is it?). For things sold by weight, ask Berapa sekilo? (how much per kilo?). Answers come fast, so learn the numbers first. If you did not catch the reply, Bisa diulang? (can you repeat that?) buys you a slower second chance.

Sizes, colours and trying things

For clothes and footwear: Ada yang lebih besar? (do you have a bigger one?) and Ada yang lebih kecil? (a smaller one?). Common colours: merah (red), biru (blue), hijau (green), hitam (black), putih (white) and kuning (yellow). Ask Bisa dicoba? (can I try it on?) before heading for the fitting room.

Bargaining, politely

In markets, gentle bargaining is expected and even enjoyed. Open with the classic Bisa kurang? (can it be less?) or Harganya bisa turun? (can the price come down?). Naming your price is fine: Lima puluh ribu, ya? (fifty thousand, okay?). Accept with Baik, saya ambil ini (okay, I'll take this), or decline kindly with Belum dulu, terima kasih (not yet, thank you) and a smile — the door stays open.

Paying and finishing

Ask for the total with Semuanya berapa? (how much is everything?). Cash is uang tunai; most shops now accept cards and QRIS e-payments — Bisa bayar pakai QRIS? is a perfectly normal question in 2026. Small change is uang kecil, and vendors will love you for having some.

Keep this guide open on market day alongside the phrasebook, and run any tricky sentence through the translator before you set out. Bargaining in Indonesian, even imperfect Indonesian, changes the whole shopping experience.

Market manners

A few courtesies keep bargaining friendly. Greet before you touch the goods, handle fabrics and fruit gently, and never begin a negotiation you would not happily finish — agreeing a price and then walking away is poor form. If a price is truly beyond you, a smile and Maaf, terlalu mahal untuk saya (sorry, too expensive for me) ends things warmly. And when a bargain succeeds, both sides should leave smiling: that, in an Indonesian market, is the sign of a fair price.

Emergency

Emergency and Health Phrases in Indonesian

Nobody plans an emergency, but a few memorised lines can make a frightening moment manageable. Indonesia has good hospitals in every major city and helpful people everywhere; these phrases make sure you can ask for the right help quickly, for yourself or for someone else. Save this page offline before you travel.

Calling for help

The word that cuts through noise is Tolong! (help!). Add Ada kecelakaan! (there has been an accident!) if there has been a crash, and Berhenti! (stop!) to halt someone or something urgently. Indonesia's general emergency number is 112, with police on 110 and ambulance or medical help on 119 — numbers worth saving in your phone alongside these words.

Getting medical help

The essentials: Di mana rumah sakit? (where is the hospital?), Panggil dokter (please call a doctor), and Panggil ambulans (call an ambulance). The word for a doctor is dokter and for a hospital, rumah sakit — literally "house of the sick", one of Indonesian's most logical names. A 24-hour emergency department is the UGD (Unit Gawat Darurat); signs will point you there.

Describing what is wrong

Start with Saya merasa tidak enak badan (I feel unwell). The word sakit means sick, ill and painful all at once, and it combines with body parts: sakit kepala (headache), sakit perut (stomach ache), sakit gigi (toothache). Other common words: demam (fever), batuk (cough), darah (blood) and alergi (allergy). "It hurts here" is Di sini sakit — point as you say it.

At the pharmacy

Medicine is obat, and a pharmacy is an apotek. Useful lines: Ada obat untuk demam? (do you have medicine for fever?) and Sehari berapa kali diminum? (how many times a day should this be taken?). Pharmacists in Indonesia are knowledgeable and often the fastest route to sensible advice for minor problems.

Police and personal safety

For theft or trouble: Panggil polisi (call the police). "I have lost my bag" is Tas saya hilang, and "I don't understand" — Saya tidak mengerti — is the honest phrase that slows any difficult conversation down to a manageable speed. The traffic warning Hati-hati! (be careful!) is one you will both hear and use often.

Grammar Basics

Indonesian Question Words and Pronouns: Ask Anything

Questions are the engine of conversation. Once you can ask what, who, where, when, why and how in Indonesian — and answer with I, you, we and they — you are no longer reciting phrases; you are talking. This guide collects the whole toolkit in one place.

The seven question words

Learn these first: apa (what), siapa (who), di mana (where), kapan (when), mengapa or its everyday cousin kenapa (why), bagaimana (how — shortened to gimana in casual speech) and berapa (how much, how many). Indonesian questions usually keep the same word order as statements, so building them is easy: take a sentence, swap in the question word, and add a rising tone.

Your first real questions

Watch the toolkit work: Ini apa? (what is this?), Siapa nama Anda? (what is your name?), Di mana toilet? (where is the toilet?), Kapan berangkat? (when do we leave?), Kenapa mahal? (why is it expensive?), Bagaimana caranya? (how is it done?) and Berapa harganya? (how much is it?). Seven questions, and half of travel life is covered.

Yes-no questions

To turn a statement into a yes-no question, Indonesians often simply raise their tone at the end — Sudah makan? (have you eaten?) is also a classic friendly greeting. For careful or formal questions, put apakah at the front: Apakah Anda sudah makan?. The small word -kah attached to the first word does the same job in writing.

The pronouns

Now the people words. "I" is saya in neutral and polite speech, and aku among family and close friends. "You" is Anda (polite, used in writing and announcements), kamu (friendly, to equals and younger people) — or simply the person's title, Pak or Bu, which is often the most respectful choice of all. "He" and "she" are the same word, dia — Indonesian does not split pronouns by gender, one less thing to remember. "They" is mereka.

Kami and kita: the two words for "we"

Indonesian has one famous extra: two words for "we". Kami means we-but-not-you (I and others, excluding the listener); kita means we-including-you. So Kita pergi (let's go — all of us) is an invitation, while Kami pergi (we are going — without you) is a farewell. Getting this right makes you sound thoughtful, because the word itself includes or excludes the person you are talking to.

Possession is easy

To say "my", "your" or "their", simply add the pronoun after the thing: rumah saya (my house), nama kamu (your name), keluarga mereka (their family). No apostrophes, no extra forms. With these words and the phrasebook, you can already build hundreds of real sentences — try a few in the translator and see how the tool handles them.

Culture

Indonesian Festivals and Culture: Words and Wishes

Indonesia is home to hundreds of ethnic groups and six recognised religions, so the calendar is a patchwork of celebrations — and every celebration comes with its own greetings. Learning the wishes below does double duty: you understand what is happening around you, and you can join in with exactly the right words.

Idul Fitri (Lebaran): the biggest celebration of all

After the fasting month of Ramadan, Indonesia celebrates Idul Fitri, universally called Lebaran. Tens of millions travel home in the great mudik (homecoming), tables fill with ketupat rice cakes, rendang and cookies, and the whole country asks forgiveness for the year's mistakes. The greeting to know: Selamat Idul Fitri. Mohon maaf lahir dan batin — "Happy Eid. Please forgive me, outwardly and inwardly." You will also hear the shorter Minal aidin wal faizin. A handshake with both hands and a sincere maaf is the heart of the day.

Nyepi: Bali's day of silence

Bali's Hindu New Year, Nyepi, is unlike anything else on Earth: for 24 hours the island falls silent — no travel, no lights, no work, no noise — so that evil spirits pass over an apparently empty island. The night before is the opposite: the ogoh-ogoh parade of giant papier-mâché demons. Wish Balinese friends Selamat Hari Raya Nyepi (Happy Nyepi Day), and if you are visiting, plan to stay quietly in your hotel and enjoy the most star-filled sky of the year.

Independence Day: 17 Agustus

On 17 August, Indonesia celebrates Hari Kemerdekaan (Independence Day, commemorating 1945). Red-and-white flags line every street, neighbourhoods hold games — cracker-eating contests, sack races and the famous greasy-pole climb panjat pinang — and the greeting is Dirgahayu Republik Indonesia (long live the Republic of Indonesia) or simply Selamat Hari Kemerdekaan! The word merdeka (free, independent) is also a beloved shout of joy.

Christmas, Imlek and the rest of the year

Christmas is Natal: wish Selamat Natal (Merry Christmas) and Selamat Tahun Baru (Happy New Year). Chinese New Year, Imlek, brings lion dances and the greeting Gong Xi Fa Cai. Deepavali is Galungan's Balinese Hindu cousins fill the calendar further — in Indonesia there is nearly always a celebration within reach.

Everyday culture words

A few words explain the spirit behind the festivals: gotong royong (working together for the community), silahturahmi (visiting to strengthen bonds — the real purpose of Lebaran calls), and ramah (friendly, warm). Send your wishes through the translator to check them, and browse the greetings guide for the everyday versions.

Joining in respectfully

Visitors are warmly included in most celebrations: accept Lebaran invitations and taste everything offered, dress modestly when visiting homes and places of worship, and use your right hand — or both hands — when giving and receiving greetings cards, gifts and food. During Ramadan, the greeting is Selamat berpuasa (a blessed fast), and eating discreetly around fasting friends is appreciated. A sincere attempt at the right greeting matters far more than perfect pronunciation; Indonesians will happily teach you the rest over coffee.

Speaking

How to Introduce Yourself in Indonesian

Introducing yourself well is the fastest way to turn strangers into friends, and in Indonesian it is refreshingly simple: no verb conjugations to juggle, no gendered words to trip over — just a few friendly sentence frames. Build yours piece by piece below, then practise the whole thing until it flows.

Step 1 — Greet and say your name

Open with the greeting for the time of day — Selamat pagi — then the magic frame: Nama saya... (my name is...). To ask back, use Siapa nama Anda? (what is your name?) or, more casually, Nama kamu siapa?. Add Senang berkenalan dengan Anda (pleased to meet you) and the introduction is already a success.

Step 2 — Say where you are from

The frame is Saya dari... (I am from...) plus your town or country: Saya dari London, Saya dari Pakistan. If you live somewhere else now, add Sekarang saya tinggal di... (now I live in...). Indonesians love geography small talk, so expect happy follow-up questions about your home.

Step 3 — Age, family and the personal details

Age uses the word umur or usia: Umur saya tiga puluh tahun (I am thirty years old — literally "my age is thirty years"). Family lines are simple: Saya sudah menikah (I am married), Saya punya dua anak (I have two children), Saya anak pertama (I am the eldest child). Our family guide supplies the rest of the vocabulary.

Step 4 — Work or studies

Your job fits one frame: Saya bekerja sebagai... (I work as a...) — guru (teacher), dokter (doctor), pedagang (trader), insinyur (engineer). Students say Saya mahasiswa (I am a university student) or Saya masih sekolah (I am still at school). And the traveller's classic: Saya sedang berlibur (I am on holiday).

Step 5 — A full worked example

Here is a complete introduction, the kind you could give at a neighbourhood gathering: Selamat sore. Nama saya Daniel. Saya dari Inggris, dan sekarang saya tinggal di Jakarta. Umur saya dua puluh delapan tahun. Saya bekerja sebagai guru bahasa Inggris. Saya senang belajar bahasa Indonesia. — "Good afternoon. My name is Daniel. I am from England, and now I live in Jakarta. I am twenty-eight years old. I work as an English teacher. I enjoy learning Indonesian." That last sentence, by the way, is the one that wins the room.

Step 6 — Keep the conversation going

Finish with an invitation to talk: Bahasa Indonesia saya masih sedikit (my Indonesian is still limited) said with a smile invites patience and help. Then ask your new friend a question from our questions guide — conversation is a two-way introduction.

Draft your own version in the translator, checking each line, and learn it by heart. Five honest sentences in Indonesian will open more doors in Indonesia than a perfect accent ever will.

Food & Drink

Indonesian for Food Lovers: Ordering Food in Indonesia

Ask Indonesians what they miss most abroad and the answer is never the weather — it is the food. Eating is the national hobby, and a little food Indonesian turns every meal into an adventure: smoky satay stalls, banana-leaf Padang feasts, and the humble warung that serves the best lunch in town. This guide gives you the words.

The places you will eat

The warung is Indonesia's beloved small eatery — cheap, fast and often superb. A rumah makan is a sit-down restaurant, a restoran a fancier one, and a kaki lima (literally "five legs") is a street cart, named for the vendor's two legs plus the cart's three. The travelling soup cart announces itself with a knock on a wooden block; follow the sound.

The dishes everyone must know

Rice is the centre of the meal: nasi is cooked rice, and a meal without it is, in the famous local saying, not yet a meal. The classics: nasi goreng (fried rice, the national comfort dish), mie goreng (fried noodles), sate (skewers grilled over charcoal — spelled satay in English — served with peanut sauce), rendang (beef slow-braised in coconut and spices, regularly voted among the world's best dishes), soto (fragrant soup, with a version in every region), gado-gado (vegetables with peanut sauce) and bakso (meatball soup). In a Padang restaurant, dishes arrive all at once in small plates; you pay for what you touch.

Mastering the spice scale

Chilli — cabai, and its fiery paste sambal — is a way of life. Protect yourself or challenge yourself with precision: tidak pedas (not spicy), sedikit pedas (a little spicy), pedas sedang (medium spicy) and, for the brave, pedas sekali (very spicy). The praise every cook wants to hear is Enak sekali! (delicious!).

Ordering like a regular

Order with Saya pesan... (I would like to order...) or the shorter Minta... (please bring...): Minta nasi goreng satu, tidak pedas (one fried rice, not spicy). Drinks: es teh (iced tea, the default refreshment), es jeruk (iced orange), kopi (coffee — ask for kopi tubruk for the strong local style) and air mineral (bottled water). Finish with Minta tagihannya (the bill, please).

Dietary phrases

Vegetarians: Saya vegetarian and the clearer Tanpa daging, ya (without meat, please). Many Indonesians eat halal; pork is daging babi, beef daging sapi, chicken ayam and fish ikan. For allergies, use the emergency guide's health phrases, and draft anything complicated in the translator before the meal.

Selamat makan — enjoy your meal. In Indonesia, you are about to, many times a day.

Tea, coffee and the culture of ngopi

Drinking together is its own ritual. Ngopi — literally "coffee-ing" — means sitting down for coffee and conversation, and an invitation to ngopi is an invitation to friendship. Sweet tea arrives with most meals whether ordered or not, ginger drinks like wedang jahe warm the rainy season, and in coffee regions from Gayo to Toraja, asking how the beans were grown starts wonderful conversations. When in doubt, accept the drink: in Indonesia, hospitality is a language of its own.

Grammar Basics

How Indonesian Sentences Are Built: Word Order and Verb Prefixes

Indonesian grammar has a reputation as the friendliest in Asia, and it is earned. There are no verb conjugations, no tenses to memorise, no genders and no plural endings. In their place stand a handful of elegant devices — time words and verb prefixes — that this guide explains one by one. Understand these, and Indonesian stops being a phrase list and becomes a language you can build with.

Word order: familiar, with one twist

Basic Indonesian sentences follow English order: subject, verb, object. Saya makan nasi — I eat rice. Dia membaca buku — she reads a book. The twist is that descriptions come after the thing described: rumah besar (house big — a big house), mobil merah (car red), orang Indonesia (person Indonesia — an Indonesian person). Once your ear flips that one switch, whole sentences fall into place.

Time without tenses: sudah, sedang, akan

The verb itself never changes. Instead, small words placed before it carry the time: sudah means the action is complete — Saya sudah makan (I have eaten); sedang means it is in progress — Saya sedang makan (I am eating); akan points to the future — Saya akan makan (I will eat). Add time words like kemarin (yesterday) or besok (tomorrow) and the system covers everything English tenses do, with a fraction of the effort.

The verb prefixes: me-, ber-, di-

Many Indonesian verbs are built from a root plus a prefix. The prefix me- (which changes shape to mem-, men-, meng- or meny- to flow smoothly) makes active verbs: from baca (read) comes membaca; from tulis (write), menulis. The prefix ber- makes verbs of having, wearing or doing: berjalan (to walk, from jalan — road), bekerja (to work). And di- builds the passive: Buku itu dibaca oleh guru (the book is read by the teacher). Indonesians use the passive a great deal — it is a polite, graceful way to keep the focus on the thing, not the person.

Saying "not" and making plurals

Negation uses two words: tidak before verbs and adjectives — Saya tidak tahu (I don't know) — and bukan before nouns — Ini bukan buku saya (this is not my book). Plurals are usually left to context, or made by doubling: buku-buku (books), anak-anak (children). Doubling also softens some words — jalan-jalan is not "roads" but a leisurely stroll.

Putting it together

Watch every device work in one sentence: Kemarin, adik saya sedang membaca buku-buku baru di rumah besar itu. — "Yesterday, my younger sibling was reading new books in that big house." Time word, sedang for the ongoing action, a doubled plural, descriptions after nouns. Build your own sentences in the translator and check them against this pattern; pair this guide with our questions and pronouns guide and you hold the complete beginner's grammar of Indonesian.

Last updated: 2026. This page explains what information Translate English to Indonesian does and does not collect.

Text you translate

Text you enter into the translator is sent to an online translation service (Google Translate and, as a fallback, MyMemory) solely to produce your translation. We do not operate servers that store your translated text, and we do not sell it. Your recent translations list is stored only in your own browser's local storage, on your device, and you can clear it at any time by clearing your browser data.

Cookies and analytics

This site may use Google Analytics to understand anonymous, aggregate usage — for example which guides are read most — so we can improve the site. Analytics cookies do not identify you personally. Advertising partners, where ads are shown, may use cookies as described in their own policies.

Third-party services

Translation requests are processed by third-party providers under their own privacy policies. Links to external sites are provided for convenience; we are not responsible for their content or privacy practices.

Contact

Questions about this policy can be sent via the contact page.
